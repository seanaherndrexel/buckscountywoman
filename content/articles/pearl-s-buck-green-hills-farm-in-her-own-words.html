On December 10, 1938, a 46-year-old novelist who lived on a farm in Hilltown Township stood up at a banquet in Stockholm City Hall and told the room that the Nobel Prize she had just received was, in her words, "a prize not so much for what has been done, as for the future."

Pearl S. Buck had 34 more years to make good on that sentence, and she spent most of them in Bucks County. This profile is assembled from her own published speeches and from the public record: the Nobel Foundation, the Pulitzer Prizes, the National Park Service, two university archives, the Michener Art Museum and Pearl S. Buck International. We did not interview anyone for it, and every quotation below links to where it was published.

A childhood on the other side of the world

The Nobel Foundation's biography gives the outline. Buck was born in 1892 in Hillsboro, West Virginia, and grew up in China, where her parents were missionaries. She graduated from Randolph-Macon Woman's College, went back to China, and lived there until 1934 apart from a year at Cornell University, where she took a master's degree in 1926.

West Virginia University, which holds her literary manuscripts, adds the family record. She married John Lossing Buck in 1917. Their daughter Carol was born in 1920 and was later diagnosed with phenylketonuria, known as PKU. Buck wrote about Carol decades afterward in The Child Who Never Grew, published in 1950, at a time when few public figures discussed intellectual disability in their own families.

Her first novel, East Wind, West Wind, appeared in 1930. The Good Earth followed in 1931. The Pulitzer Prizes' own account says she wrote it in 1929 while living in Nanking, that it was her second novel, and that the 1932 jury's choice was unanimous.

The storyteller in the village tent

Two days after the banquet, on December 12, 1938, Buck delivered her Nobel lecture. She called it "The Chinese Novel," and she used it to say plainly where her craft came from. "My earliest knowledge of story, of how to tell and write stories, came to me in China," she said. And then: "it is the Chinese and not the American novel which has shaped my own efforts in writing."

The lecture is also her clearest statement of who she thought a novelist works for. She described the Chinese novelist as "a storyteller in a village tent" who has to win an audience one listener at a time, and she closed with a standard she applied to herself. A novelist, she said, "must be satisfied if the common people hear him gladly. At least, so I have been taught in China."

Critics did not always return the compliment. The National Park Service nomination form for her house, prepared in 1979, says flatly that American literary critics largely dismissed her, and it rests her significance on her enormous readership, on how she interpreted China for Western readers and on her humanitarian work.

"Given to a woman"

Her banquet speech runs only a few paragraphs, and she spent one of them on a fact the audience could see for itself. "In my country it is important that this award has been given to a woman," she said. West Virginia University's archive notes that she remains one of only two American women to win the literature prize. The other is Toni Morrison.

She was candid about her own country in the same speech. "We are a people still young," she told the Swedish audience, "and we know that we have not yet come to the fullest of our powers." She called China her "foster country." And she named the idea she said connected the two places: "Freedom," she said, "is today more than ever the most precious human possession."

A stone house on Dublin Road

By then she had a home address in Bucks County. The sources don't agree on the exact year she arrived. The National Park Service form says she lived at Green Hills Farm from 1933 until her death in 1973, the University of Pennsylvania's biography says she moved there in 1934, and the Michener Art Museum's Bucks County Artists Database says 1935, the year she married publisher Richard Walsh. All three place her there by the middle of the decade.

The Park Service form describes what she bought: a two-and-a-half-story fieldstone farmhouse built in 1835, with an 1827 barn and a stone summer kitchen that predates the Revolution. Buck added a stone wing and two single-story libraries, stretching the house to about 135 feet. The Michener database says the rolling hills and stone houses reminded her of China, and that she raised ten children in the county. Penn's biography counts Janice, adopted in the 1920s, and six more children adopted with Walsh.

What "the future" turned out to hold

In 1949 she founded Welcome House. Penn's biography calls it the first international, interracial adoption agency. Pearl S. Buck International, the organization that carries on her work from the same property, says the program matched more than 7,000 children with adoptive families in the United States between 1949 and 2014. West Virginia University's archive names the first two children placed: David and Leon Yoder.

In 1964 she set up the Pearl S. Buck Foundation to support children who would stay in their own countries, funded by sponsorship. The two efforts later merged. She died on March 6, 1973, and is buried at Green Hills Farm. The house is a National Historic Landmark.

Walking through it this fall

The Pearl S. Buck House is at 520 Dublin Road, Perkasie, PA 18944. The phone number is (215) 249-0100. Docent-led tours run Tuesday to Saturday at 11 a.m., 1 p.m. and 2 p.m., and Sunday at 1 p.m. and 2 p.m. There are no tours on Mondays. Tickets are $15 for adults, $12 for seniors and $7 for students, and members get in free. Reservations are recommended.

Three things on the calendar are worth knowing about now.

Through October 18: quilts in every room

"Pieced Together: American Quilts Across Generations" fills the rooms of the house with 163 years of quilts made by women, from 19th-century bed quilts to contemporary art quilts. It closes Sunday, October 18. The 11 a.m. and 2 p.m. slots combine the house's Life and Legacy tour with the exhibit, and the 1 p.m. slot is the exhibit alone.

October evenings: ghost tours

Ghost tours of the house start at 6:30 p.m. on Sunday, October 18, Saturday, October 24 and Saturday, October 31. Tickets are $18 for adults, $15 for seniors 65 and older and $12 for students 13 to 18. The organization doesn't recommend them for children under 13.

From November 10: the 50th Festival of Trees

The house's Festival of Trees marks its 50th anniversary this year. It begins Tuesday, November 10, and tour tickets for it are being sold for dates through January 3, 2027. The house is closed on Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Eve, Christmas Day, New Year's Eve and New Year's Day.

For readers who would rather start with the books, a free online discussion of Buck's novel Dragon Seed meets Tuesday, October 13, from 10 to 11:30 a.m. Register by calling the main number.

Quick answers

Where exactly is the Pearl S. Buck House?

It's at 520 Dublin Road in Hilltown Township, with a Perkasie mailing address, southwest of Dublin borough. The property is also the headquarters of Pearl S. Buck International.

Is the house accessible for visitors who use a wheelchair or walker?

Not fully. The organization says there are stairs throughout the museum tour and that most areas of the house aren't easily reached with a wheelchair or walking support device. It offers a virtual tour on request, so call ahead and ask.

Can I visit without taking the tour?

Yes, in part. The organization says the gardens can be visited without buying a museum tour. The gift shop is open Tuesday to Friday 9 a.m. to 4 p.m., Saturday 10 a.m. to 3 p.m. and Sunday noon to 3 p.m.

Where can I read Buck's Nobel speeches for myself?

Both are free on NobelPrize.org. The banquet speech takes about two minutes to read. The lecture, "The Chinese Novel," is much longer and is the better guide to how she thought about her own work.
