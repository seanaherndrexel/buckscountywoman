On February 12, 2026, the FDA approved new labeling for six menopausal hormone therapy products, and the boxed warnings about cardiovascular disease, breast cancer and probable dementia were gone. Those warnings had sat at the top of hormone therapy labels for more than two decades. For anyone who has been told, or has quietly assumed, that hormone therapy was off the table, that single regulatory action changes the starting point of the conversation.

It wasn't the only shift. In the last two years a new class of non-hormonal drugs has grown to two approved options, national breast cancer screening guidance moved earlier, and the professional society that certifies menopause clinicians has been pushing for care that fits the individual rather than a blanket rule. This piece walks through what changed, what the evidence says, and where women and people experiencing menopause, including surgical or medical menopause, can find knowledgeable care in Bucks County.

This article is health information, not medical advice. Talk with your clinician about what's right for you.

A warning label rewritten after 20-plus years

The story starts on November 10, 2025, when the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services and the FDA announced they would begin removing the broad boxed warnings from hormone therapy products. The agency said the original warnings grew out of the Women's Health Initiative study from the early 2000s, where the average participant was 63, more than a decade past the typical onset of menopause, and took hormone formulations that are no longer commonly used. An FDA expert panel had met in July 2025 to review the literature.

The HHS fact sheet spelled out the details. Boxed warning language on cardiovascular disease, breast cancer and probable dementia would come off. The boxed warning for endometrial cancer would stay, but only on systemic estrogen-alone products. That matters for anyone with a uterus, since estrogen without a progestogen raises the risk of endometrial cancer. The updated labels also point toward timing, recommending that systemic therapy be started within 10 years of menopause onset or before age 60, while leaving the final decision to the patient and prescriber.

Three months later, the FDA approved the first six revised labels, covering all four categories of hormone therapy: combined estrogen and progestogen, systemic estrogen alone, systemic progestogen alone for women with a uterus, and low-dose vaginal estrogen. The agency stated that women who start hormone therapy within 10 years of menopause onset, generally before 60, show a reduction in all-cause mortality and fractures.

How clinicians responded

The Menopause Society, the North American professional body formerly known as NAMS, supported removing the boxed warning from low-dose vaginal estrogen, saying it may have discouraged use of a safe and effective treatment for genitourinary symptoms like dryness, painful sex and urinary issues. On systemic estrogen, the Society was more measured. It said risks are low for younger, healthy women starting closer to menopause and greater when therapy begins in older women, and that medical history, family history, symptoms and personal preference should all shape the decision.

Research published this year in the Society's journal, Menopause, underlines that point about timing. A study of 83,147 women titled "Health outcomes of hormone therapy initiated or continued after age 65" found higher hazards for cancer and stroke with late initiation, and the authors concluded that starting hormone therapy late should be discouraged. The label changed. Age and personal risk still count.

Two pills that work on the brain, not the ovaries

For people who can't take hormones, or don't want to, the bigger change is pharmacological. Hot flashes and night sweats are driven in part by a group of neurons in the hypothalamus, the brain's temperature control center. When estrogen falls, signaling through neurokinin receptors in that area becomes overactive. A newer class of drugs blocks those receptors directly.

Fezolinetant (Veozah)

The FDA approved fezolinetant on May 12, 2023 as a 45 mg daily pill that blocks the NK3 receptor. It was the first drug of its kind. In December 2024, the agency added a boxed warning for rare but serious liver injury, prompted in part by a postmarketing report of a patient who developed signs of liver injury about 40 days after starting the drug. The label now calls for liver blood tests before treatment begins and on a set schedule during the first nine months, and for stopping the drug if symptoms of liver injury appear.

Elinzanetant (Lynkuet)

Bayer's elinzanetant won FDA approval on October 24, 2025. It is the first approved drug to block both NK1 and NK3 receptors. The evidence comes from the OASIS program. In OASIS 1 and 2, published in JAMA in October 2024, roughly 800 postmenopausal women aged 40 to 65 took 120 mg daily or placebo. Those on the drug had about three fewer moderate to severe hot flashes per day than the placebo group at both week 4 and week 12, along with improvements in sleep disturbance and menopause-related quality of life. OASIS 3 followed participants for 52 weeks to assess long-term safety.

According to its prescribing information, Lynkuet is taken as two 60 mg capsules at bedtime and carries no boxed warning. Its warnings cover sleepiness and other nervous system effects (reported in 11.9% of patients versus 3.5% on placebo), liver enzyme elevations that call for testing at baseline and at three months, a seizure precaution, and a contraindication in pregnancy. The most common side effects were headache, fatigue, dizziness and sleepiness.

One more development is pending. On September 28, 2026, Bayer announced that the FDA accepted its application and granted Priority Review for using elinzanetant to treat moderate to severe hot flashes caused by endocrine therapy for breast cancer. The phase 3 OASIS-4 trial enrolled 474 women with hormone receptor-positive breast cancer and met its primary endpoints at weeks 4 and 12. Bayer notes that no FDA-approved treatment currently exists for this group, which includes many people whose menopause was brought on by cancer treatment.

The rest of the non-hormonal toolkit

The Menopause Society's 2023 nonhormone therapy position statement remains the Society's current guidance on this topic. It recommends cognitive behavioral therapy, clinical hypnosis, SSRI and SNRI antidepressants, gabapentin and fezolinetant at its highest evidence level, plus oxybutynin, weight loss and stellate ganglion block with supporting evidence. It does not recommend supplements and herbal remedies, soy products, cannabinoids, acupuncture or paced breathing for hot flashes, based on the evidence it reviewed.

Screening moved earlier, and density got a disclosure rule

Midlife care isn't only about symptoms. On April 30, 2024, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force finalized its recommendation that women get a screening mammogram every other year from age 40 through 74, a grade B recommendation. The previous guidance left screening in the 40s to individual choice. The Task Force said evidence remains insufficient on screening after 75 and on supplemental ultrasound or MRI for dense breasts.

Density still matters for the conversation. Since September 10, 2024, FDA rules under the Mammography Quality Standards Act require mammography facilities to tell patients whether their breast tissue is dense. If your report says it is, that's a reason to ask your clinician whether anything beyond a standard mammogram makes sense for you.

Finding care in Bucks County

The county's hospital map has changed too. Doylestown Health joined Penn Medicine on April 1, 2025 and now operates as Penn Medicine Doylestown Health. Grand View Health in Sellersville became part of St. Luke's University Health Network on July 1, 2025.

St. Luke's Menopause Specialists

Among the systems serving Bucks County, St. Luke's has the most clearly defined menopause offering we could verify. Its Menopause Specialists program covers perimenopause through postmenopause and runs a small-group education series called Menopause & Me. A September 2025 network post named six Menopause Society Certified Practitioners and described extended consultations, typically about an hour, with access to weight management, pelvic floor physical therapy, urogynecology and behavioral health. Most of those certified clinicians practice in the Lehigh Valley, but one of them, Ann Freeman, DO, is listed at St. Luke's Valley GYN Associates in Quakertown. The program's scheduling line is 484-526-8600.

Penn Medicine Doylestown Health and others

Penn Medicine lists menopause and postmenopause care within its gynecology service. As of this writing, we did not find a separately named menopause program at the Doylestown campus on its public pages, so call the OB/GYN office directly and ask whether any clinicians hold the MSCP credential. The same goes for Jefferson Health's Bucks County OB/GYN offices and Trinity Health Mid-Atlantic's St. Mary practices in Langhorne.

The credential to ask about

The letters to look for are MSCP, for Menopause Society Certified Practitioner. The Society's Find a Menopause Practitioner tool searches by ZIP code with a radius of up to 50 miles. It isn't complete, because it only includes members and certified practitioners who chose to be listed, so a clinician who doesn't appear may still hold the credential. When we searched Bucks County towns, the directory didn't return clear local listings, which is one more reason to ask the practice directly.

Walk in with a plan

A standard gynecology visit is short, and menopause conversations tend to run long. A little preparation helps you use the time well.

  • Track symptoms for two to four weeks. Note hot flashes and night sweats per day, sleep, mood, joint aches, vaginal or urinary changes, and your cycle if you still have one.
  • Bring your history. Include any surgery that removed your ovaries or uterus, cancer treatment, blood clots, stroke, liver disease, migraine and your family history of breast cancer and heart disease.
  • List every medication and supplement. Several menopause treatments interact with other drugs, and the newer non-hormonal pills require liver testing.
  • Know your screening dates. Bring your last mammogram date and whether it showed dense tissue, plus any bone density results.
  • Ask directly. Good questions include: Am I a candidate for hormone therapy given my age and history? Would vaginal estrogen help my symptoms? How do fezolinetant and elinzanetant compare for me? What monitoring would I need?
  • Request a longer slot. When you book, say the visit is for menopause care. Some practices, including St. Luke's program, schedule extended consultations.

Frequently asked questions

Did the FDA say hormone therapy is safe for everyone?

No. It removed several boxed warnings and kept the endometrial cancer warning on systemic estrogen-only products. Labels still describe risks, and the FDA's guidance focuses on starting within 10 years of menopause or before 60. Your own history decides the rest.

I had my ovaries removed. Does any of this apply to me?

Yes. Surgical and medical menopause often bring symptoms on suddenly, and the treatment options discussed here can apply. If your menopause followed cancer treatment, ask your oncology team before starting anything, since some options may not be appropriate.

What's the difference between Veozah and Lynkuet?

Both are non-hormonal daily pills for moderate to severe hot flashes. Veozah blocks one receptor (NK3) and carries a boxed warning for rare liver injury. Lynkuet blocks two (NK1 and NK3), is taken at bedtime, and has warnings for sleepiness and liver enzyme changes. Both need liver blood tests.

How do I know if a doctor really specializes in menopause?

Ask whether they hold the MSCP credential from The Menopause Society, or search the Society's directory by ZIP code. Plenty of experienced gynecologists and primary care clinicians manage menopause well without it, so it's a useful signal rather than a requirement.
