Below 5 percent. That's the share of breast cancers found by screening that a new analysis says may be "overdiagnosed," meaning they would never have caused harm in a woman's lifetime. Older estimates drawn from the same clinical trials ran as high as 30 to 50 percent.

The paper appeared in September in JNCI: Journal of the National Cancer Institute, three weeks before Breast Cancer Awareness Month began. It doesn't change any screening guideline. It does speak to a fear that keeps some women from booking a mammogram, so here is what the researchers did, what they didn't prove, and where screening and risk assessment are available in Bucks County.

Eight old trials, read again

The study is a meta-analysis titled "Breast cancer overdiagnosis in mammography trials: separating signal from noise." Its authors are Sisse Helle Njor, Casper Urth Pedersen, Elsebeth Lynge, Robert A. Smith and Matejka Rebolj, working from the University of Southern Denmark, the University of Copenhagen, Queen Mary University of London and the American Cancer Society.

They reanalyzed all eight randomized trials of mammography screening: the New York Health Insurance Plan trial, Malmö, Two-County, Edinburgh, the Canadian National Breast Screening Study, Stockholm, Gothenburg and UK Age. They counted both invasive cancers and ductal carcinoma in situ.

The argument turns on timing. When screening starts, diagnoses jump, because tumors are found a few years sooner than they would have surfaced on their own. If researchers stop counting too early, that jump looks like extra cancers. The team adjusted for three things: whether women in the comparison group were later screened too, how many rounds of screening each group was offered, and how long the women were followed.

For a yardstick they used Denmark, where organized screening began in some regions 17 years before others. According to the University of Southern Denmark's summary, the excess cases in the trials closely matched what Danish routine screening would predict, and overdiagnosis there is estimated at below 5 percent. Rebolj said the trial data fit overdiagnosis of less than 5 percent "rather than with estimates nearing 50%."

The fine print on that number

"Consistent with" is careful wording. The study doesn't measure overdiagnosis directly in American women, and it leans on Danish data as its reference. The university summary we read doesn't give confidence intervals or per-trial figures, so we're not reporting any.

U.S. guidance uses different math. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, in its April 30, 2024 recommendation, says trials with comparable groups put overdiagnosis at roughly 11 to 19 percent of diagnosed cancers. Its modeling estimates 14 overdiagnosed cases for every 1,000 women screened every other year from 40 to 74, with a range of 4 to 37. That recommendation stands, and the new paper hasn't altered it.

Two of the five authors reported research funding, one from the Novo Nordisk Foundation and one from Cancer Research UK. One author works for the American Cancer Society, which recommends screening.

Lead author Njor's own summary is modest. Most women will not develop breast cancer, she said, and the benefit of finding it early likely outweighs a small risk of unnecessary treatment. Whether that holds for you is a conversation for you and your clinician. This article is information, not medical advice.

Where the guidelines sit this October

The Task Force recommends a screening mammogram every two years from age 40 through 74, a grade B recommendation. It applies to cisgender women and to anyone assigned female at birth, including transgender men and nonbinary people, at average risk. It doesn't cover people with a BRCA1 or BRCA2 variant, prior breast cancer, or high-dose chest radiation at a young age, who need a separate plan.

Other groups read the evidence differently. The Society of Breast Imaging restated this month that it advises yearly mammograms from 40. Local hospital pages, including St. Luke's, say the same.

On dense breasts, the Task Force says the evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against extra ultrasound or MRI after a normal mammogram. Density still belongs in the conversation. Under a Food and Drug Administration rule issued in March 2023, every mammography facility must tell you whether your breasts are dense. The FDA says about half of women over 40 have dense tissue.

The bill for the second look

Pennsylvania's Act 1 of 2023 requires state-regulated insurance plans to cover supplemental breast MRI and ultrasound, plus BRCA-related genetic counseling and testing, at no cost for women at high risk. The PA Breast Cancer Coalition says all such plans have applied it since January 1, 2025. Qualifying factors include a personal or family history of breast cancer, a history of abnormal screenings, or extremely dense breasts. Heterogeneously dense tissue qualifies when paired with another factor, such as a lifetime risk above 20 percent.

Self-insured employer plans and Medicare follow federal rules, so call your insurer before you book. A second law, Act 52 of 2025, extends no-cost coverage to diagnostic imaging after an abnormal finding, with full implementation due by January 2028.

Quick answers

What does overdiagnosis mean?

It means screening finds a cancer that would never have caused symptoms or shortened a woman's life. Doctors usually can't tell which cancers those are, so they get treated. It's different from a false positive, which is an abnormal result that turns out not to be cancer.

Did the new study change when I should get a mammogram?

No. The Task Force still recommends screening every two years from 40 to 74 for people at average risk, and several medical societies advise yearly screening. The study addresses one harm of screening, and guideline panels haven't acted on it.

How do I find out whether my breasts are dense?

Read the results letter from your last mammogram. Federal rules require the facility to state whether your tissue is dense. If you can't find the letter, ask the imaging center or your doctor's office for the report.

Can I get a mammogram in Bucks County without a doctor's order?

Plan on needing one. Jefferson-Solis and Grand View both say an order or prescription is required, and St. Mary says a referral may be needed. If you're uninsured or underinsured, the state's Breast and Cervical Cancer Early Detection Program may cover screening and follow-up.
