Women own 394,464 businesses in Pennsylvania, and 354,000 of them have no employees. Those figures come from the U.S. Small Business Administration's Office of Advocacy, in its 2024 state profile. Put another way, roughly nine in ten women-owned firms in the state are one woman doing the selling, the bookkeeping and the work itself.

That's a lot of people with nobody down the hall to ask. Bucks County has more free help for them than most owners realize, and a fair amount of it sits within a few miles of the Doylestown courthouse. We checked each program below against the organization's own pages this week.

A mentor who doesn't send a bill

SCORE is a national nonprofit of volunteer business advisors and a resource partner of the SBA. The local chapter, now called SCORE Greater Philadelphia, lists its office at 11 Welden Drive, Suite 100, in Doylestown, phone (215) 943-8850. It covers Bucks, Chester, Delaware, Montgomery and Philadelphia counties, along with parts of the Lehigh Valley and nearby New Jersey.

One-on-one mentoring is free, and the chapter says it stays free for the life of your business. You request a mentor through the website and can meet more than once.

The chapter also runs online classes. Many cost nothing and the rest are cheap. Its October calendar includes:

  • Funding and Cash Flow Strategies, Tuesday, October 13, noon, free
  • Creating a Quick Business Plan, Thursday, October 15, 6 p.m., $10
  • Setting Up a New Business Entity, Monday, October 19, 6 p.m., $10
  • Bookkeeping for Small Business, Thursday, October 22, noon, $10

A $35 certificate option bundles five of the October evening and lunchtime webinars. SCORE's national calendar adds a free session on Thursday, October 22, at 1 p.m., called When Banks Say No: Alternative Funding Options for Small Businesses.

The consultants at the biotech center

Pennsylvania's Small Business Development Centers are assigned by county, and the assignment surprises people. Temple University's center serves Philadelphia and Montgomery counties. Bucks belongs to the Lehigh University SBDC, which covers Lehigh, Northampton and Bucks.

You don't have to drive to Bethlehem. Lehigh keeps a Bucks County office at the Pennsylvania Biotechnology Center, 3805 Old Easton Road, Doylestown, open by appointment only. The main number is (610) 758-3980 and the email is insbdc@lehigh.edu.

The difference between SCORE and the SBDC is mostly who sits across the table. SCORE mentors are volunteers, many of them retired owners and executives. SBDC consultants are paid staff. Lehigh describes its consulting as confidential and no-cost.

The piece most worth knowing about is Lehigh's financing assistance. Consultants explain bank and government loan programs, help you work out which one fits, and help prepare the loan package itself, including cash flow projections. The center is blunt on one point: the SBA makes and backs loans. It doesn't hand out grants to start or expand a business.

Loans priced below the bank's

The Bucks County Economic Development Corporation is a nonprofit lender at 2003 S. Easton Road, Suite 308, Doylestown, phone 215-348-9031. It packages state-backed loans for county businesses.

BCEDC has described two programs aimed at smaller companies. Its Business Builder Loan Fund is for small and emerging Bucks County firms and can pay for land, buildings, expansion, and machinery and equipment. Its Diverse Business Financing Initiative, run with the Pennsylvania Industrial Development Authority, offers low-interest loans to qualifying businesses owned by women, people of color, veterans and LGBT owners, among others. That one can also cover working capital. Both programs are tied to creating or keeping full-time jobs, so they suit a business that's hiring more than a solo practice.

PIDA loans can't be applied for directly. They go through a certified local organization such as BCEDC. The state resets PIDA rates every quarter. For applications received July 1 to September 30, 2026, the small business real estate loan of up to $400,000 carried a fixed 5 percent rate, and equipment loans were fixed at 5.5 percent for up to 10 years. PIDA generally finances up to half of a project's cost and expects one full-time job created or kept for every $75,000 borrowed. A new quarter began October 1, so ask BCEDC for the current rate sheet before you run numbers.

Smaller sums, down to $1,000

Not every business needs six figures. The Women's Opportunities Resource Center, a Philadelphia nonprofit and SBA microloan intermediary, lists direct loans of $1,000 to $2,500, small business loans up to $10,000, a credit line up to $20,000 and expansion loans up to $50,000. Its lending line is 215-564-5500.

WORC also runs the state-funded business assistance center for the southeast region, which serves Bucks along with Philadelphia, Chester, Delaware and Montgomery counties. According to the Northeast Times, the center offers one-on-one counseling, accounting help, training and access to loan capital for businesses with fewer than 25 employees and $1 million or less in revenue.

The grant window, and when it opens

Grants exist, but they're narrow and they come in rounds. In February, the Pennsylvania Department of Community and Economic Development announced $15 million for micro-grants under its Historically Disadvantaged Business Assistance program. Four of the ten organizations it funded cover Bucks County: the Pennsylvania CDFI Network, the Chester County Economic Development Council, Greenline Access Capital and WORC. The state said WORC planned grants of $2,500 to $10,000 paired with its microloans.

The largest round has already closed. The PA CDFI Network took applications from July 9 to August 10 for grants of $7,500 to $20,000, scaled to a firm's revenue. Its rules give a sense of who qualifies: a for-profit Pennsylvania business with fewer than 100 full-time employees, at least 51 percent owned by historically disadvantaged Pennsylvania residents. We found no open application deadline this week. Calling WORC and asking to be told about its next round is the practical step.

Coursework for the price of a library card

The Bucks County Free Library gives cardholders LinkedIn Learning, the video course service with classes on spreadsheets, marketing and software, plus Value Line, Morningstar, The Wall Street Journal and The New York Times. You log in with your card number and PIN.

For something more structured, Bucks County Community College offers a 19-credit Entrepreneurship Certificate. It includes an introduction to entrepreneurship, business communication, personal financial planning, and a choice of courses in small business management, business law and social media marketing. The college says graduates should be able to write a realistic business plan. Tuition applies.

Quick answers

Which Small Business Development Center covers Bucks County?

Lehigh University's. It serves Lehigh, Northampton and Bucks counties and sees Bucks clients by appointment at the Pennsylvania Biotechnology Center, 3805 Old Easton Road, Doylestown. Call (610) 758-3980.

Is SCORE mentoring free for an established business too?

Yes. The Greater Philadelphia chapter says mentoring is free for the life of your business, so it isn't limited to startups. Some of its webinars carry a $10 fee.

Are there grants only for women-owned businesses in Bucks County?

We didn't find one open this week. The state's micro-grant program targets historically disadvantaged owners, and its biggest 2026 round closed August 10. Most of the money available to small firms here comes as low-interest loans.

What should I bring to a first meeting with a lender or consultant?

The PA CDFI Network's grant checklist is a fair guide to what any funder wants: federal and state tax returns, photo ID, your business formation and ownership papers, your EIN, and a written plan for how you'd use the money.
